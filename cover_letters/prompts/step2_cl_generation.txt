THE RECIPE — 5 principles every letter must satisfy
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PRINCIPLE 1 — THE HOOK MUST BE A SCENE, NOT A STATEMENT
The opening line must place the reader inside a specific moment, observation,
or realization. Not "I am excited to apply" or "I have always been passionate
about X." A scene: a decision being made, a number that didn't make sense,
a meeting where something clicked, a product moment that revealed a gap.
The reader should be curious after the first two sentences, not reassured.

PRINCIPLE 2 — TELL ONE STORY, NOT THREE ACHIEVEMENTS
The body of the letter is built around one story — one situation where Akshat
identified a problem that others had accepted as a given, diagnosed the real
cause, and built something that proved his diagnosis right. Do not list three
bullet-point achievements. Do not name-drop multiple projects. Depth over
breadth: a reader should be able to describe what Akshat did at one company
in one precise sentence by the end of the body paragraph.

PRINCIPLE 3 — COMPANY RESEARCH MUST GO BELOW THE SURFACE
The company paragraph cannot reference: the company's mission statement, their
homepage tagline, generic product categories, or things any applicant would
know from a 2-minute Google search. It must reference something specific: an
architectural decision, a known product tension, a customer segment insight, a
recent strategic move. If the intel section provides specific detail, use it.
If not, use the company_research_angle from the Step 1 analysis and flag that
deeper research would strengthen this section.

SPECIFICITY FLOOR FOR P3: Identifying the tension is not enough. You must name
the SPECIFIC place where that tension plays out — a product surface, a feature,
a customer segment, a metric, or a known internal trade-off. "Delinea faces a
tension between simplicity and auditability" is a FAIL. "That tension lives in
how IT teams configure role-assignment workflows during onboarding — the moment
where simplicity promises meet compliance requirements" is a PASS. One concrete
noun (a named product, a specific workflow, a measurable moment) is required.

PRINCIPLE 4 — ONE QUOTABLE LINE
Every letter must contain one sentence so precise and confident that a hiring
manager would want to share it. This is not a clever phrase for its own sake —
it's a sentence where Akshat names the real insight behind the story in a way
that sounds earned, not rehearsed. It typically lives at the end of the story
paragraph or the start of the company paragraph.
Examples of what this sounds like:
  "They weren't choosing on features — they were choosing on trust."
  "The fastest-growing segment was already in our funnel; we just had no offer
   for them."
  "Supply wasn't a resource problem. It was a product problem."
This line should make the reader pause. If you can't find one, the story is
not specific enough yet.

PRINCIPLE 5 — THE CLOSE MUST ECHO THE OPENING
If the hook was quiet and observational, the closing is too. If the hook was
high-stakes and urgent, the closing brings that energy forward. The close
should NOT be: "I look forward to hearing from you." It should either: (a)
loop back to the opening scene with a new implication, (b) make one precise
claim about what Akshat would do in the first 90 days, or (c) name one open
question about the company that he'd want to dig into on day one.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EM DASH POLICY (STRICT — ZERO ALLOWED)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The em dash (—) is FORBIDDEN. Do not use em dashes anywhere in the cover
letter — not for diagnostic splits, not for sentence connectors, not for list
separators, not for any purpose.

When you would normally use an em dash for a break or pivot, use a PERIOD and
start a new sentence. This is the preferred replacement in almost every case:
  BAD:  "The product wasn't broken — the proof layer was missing."
  GOOD: "The product wasn't broken. The proof layer was missing."
A semicolon is acceptable only for two tightly-linked independent clauses of
equal weight. Do NOT use a semicolon as a drop-in em-dash substitute after
a noun phrase (e.g., "a debugging tool ; was the artifact" is wrong).
Every em dash will be auto-replaced in post-processing, so including one only
risks awkward punctuation in the final output.

STRUCTURE — what each paragraph does
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

PARAGRAPH 1 — THE HOOK (~80–100 words)
Open with a scene, decision, or observation. Not about Akshat — about the
problem, the moment, the realization. The scene should directly connect to
the hook_angle in the Step 1 analysis. End P1 with one sentence that
implicitly or explicitly signals: "this is the problem I've been working on."

PARAGRAPH 2 — THE STORY (~130–160 words)
Tell one story from the recommended stories, adapted to resonate with this
company's context. Use the pivot structure: observation → insight → action →
scale. Include one quotable line. Numbers must be verbatim from the story bank
(never fabricate or approximate). The story should feel like it's evidence for
a thesis, not a list of credentials.

PARAGRAPH 3 — THE COMPANY CONNECTION (~70–90 words)
Connect Akshat's angle to something specific about this company — not their
mission, but a product decision, a customer segment, a known architectural
challenge, a strategic move. Use the company_research_angle from Step 1 and
any intel provided. If intel is sparse, be honest: flag in a <!-- comment -->
that this paragraph would benefit from deeper research, but still write the
strongest version you can with available information.

PARAGRAPH 4 — THE CLOSE (~40–60 words)
Echo the opening register. Choose one of:
  (a) Loop back to the opening scene with a new implication
  (b) Name one precise thing Akshat would do or dig into in the first 90 days
  (c) Ask one open question about the company that implies he's already thinking
      like an insider
Never close with "I look forward to hearing from you." Never grovel.

REGISTER RULE FOR THE CLOSE: If you use option (b) (90-day plan), the plan
must NOT end on a procedural note. It must end on the letter's core insight,
restated or sharpened. A work plan that ends with "then I would document my
findings" fails. A work plan that ends with "the question I'd want answered is
X" — where X callbacks to the hook's diagnostic register — passes. The last
sentence of the letter should feel like it could have come from the first.

TOTAL LENGTH: 320–420 words. Not including header/footer.
