Dear Cisco Strategy Team,

In an enterprise sales cycle, the question that kills a deal rarely appears in the product spec. At Hevo Data, competing against Fivetran for Fortune 500 data teams, I watched technically superior demos lose to slower, more expensive alternatives. The evaluators weren't scoring features. They were scoring confidence: could they explain a pipeline failure to their CFO at 9 a.m. on a Monday? That question lived nowhere in our roadmap, and answering it required reading both the codebase and the procurement checklist at the same time.

The diagnosis changed everything. Hevo's streaming-first architecture failed silently, which meant no audit trail and no defensible failure boundary for enterprise buyers. The real blocker wasn't speed or cost. It was verifiability. I built the strategic case to pivot toward batch-first, transactional execution: a deliberate choice to trade latency for SLA compliance and traceable failure states. The architectural argument, not the feature list, became the basis for Hevo 2.0. Within 90 days of the pivot, eight enterprise customers who had previously passed on us signed. The fastest path to enterprise isn't the fastest product. It is the one whose failure modes a CFO can describe in a board meeting.



That same diagnostic instinct is what draws me to Cisco's Corporate Strategy Office, specifically to the build-buy-partner questions your team is navigating as networking revenue commoditizes. The Splunk acquisition positioned Cisco to compete in AI-driven security operations, but the integration creates a precise stress point: how much of the Security Cloud capability stack should Cisco develop organically versus close through partnership, given that Palo Alto's XSIAM and CrowdStrike are compressing the window for differentiation? That is not a market-research question. It is a structural diagnosis, and it is exactly the kind of problem I have been solving with live stakes.

In the first 90 days, I would map the Security Cloud and Splunk integration against the three nearest competitive vectors to produce a build-buy-partner gap analysis on the capability spaces where Cisco's current portfolio is thinnest. The real question I would want to pressure-test with BU GMs: which gaps are competitively urgent, and which are gaps the market hasn't yet told customers to care about.

Sincerely,
Akshat Pathak
akshatpathakrocks@gmail.com
