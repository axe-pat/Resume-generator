Dear DwyerOmega Product Team,

During a competitive enterprise trial at Hevo Data, a data engineering lead handed back a product demo with one sentence: "I can't tell my team what happened when this fails." The pipeline was fast. The architecture was elegant. None of that mattered. The deal blocker wasn't a missing feature. It was a missing proof layer that would let a technical buyer trust the system enough to stake their job on it.

That realization shaped how I approach product data problems at every scale. At Hevo, I diagnosed the real blocker as auditability rather than performance, led the architectural shift to batch-first transactional execution, and built Job Monitoring as a first-class product surface showing exactly when pipelines failed, why, and what recovered. Within 90 days, eight enterprise customers who had previously passed on us signed contracts. The feature that closed those deals was not on the original roadmap. Visibility is a product decision. At Intuit, the same diagnostic instinct surfaced a different version of the same problem: 80,000+ QuickBooks Payroll businesses experiencing billing mismatches traced not to a bug but to a reconciliation gap across three systems never designed to talk to each other. I built the financial case to pause feature delivery, designed the cross-system reconciliation framework, and presented the revenue impact to senior leadership. Two companies, one pattern: messy or incomplete data sitting in the seam between systems quietly erodes the trust that downstream outcomes depend on.



DwyerOmega's growth through acquisition, including the Omega Engineering merger, almost certainly left attribute inconsistency distributed across legacy product lines in ways that look invisible at the catalog level but show up acutely at the point of digital search and specification. That friction lives specifically in the configure-and-compare workflow that industrial buyers use when they hit a pressure transmitter series page and find incomplete specs. Incomplete attributes at that moment do not read as a data problem. They read as a product the buyer cannot trust.

In the first 90 days, I would map attribute completeness across the Pressure and Force SKU catalog, identify which product series carry the highest search-to-conversion drop-off due to missing or inconsistent specs, and produce a data remediation brief with a governance workflow the channel team can act on. The question I would want answered by day 30 is the same one that engineering lead asked me in that demo room: what does a buyer see when something is wrong, and does the data tell them fast enough to stay?

Sincerely,
Akshat Pathak
akshatpathakrocks@gmail.com
